Abstract
Thermal annealing can modify the frozen molecular orientation, crystallization state, residual stress and inter -
road interfaces of fused -filament-fabricated (FFF) polylactic acid (PLA). These changes may improve heat
resistance or selected mechanical responses, yet the same increase in chain mobility can cause anisotropic
shrinkage, thickness growth and warpage. Published studies have examined conventional oven treatment and
support-assisted routes using sand, ceramic powder, salt, gypsum, liquids and elast omeric encapsulation, but
their conclusions are difficult to compare because PLA formulation, geometry, print architecture, temperature
regime and response definitions differ. This paper develops a feasible, standards -informed framework for a
controlled comparison of as -printed, unconstrained, sand -supported, salt-supported and mechanically fixture -
constrained PLA. A 25 -specimen screen at 95 C for 60 min is followed by a replicated 3 temperature x 3
duration x 2 constraint -state factorial, tensile confirmat ion and limited flexural confirmation, for 130 physical
specimens. Primary responses are ultimate tensile strength, three -axis dimensional change and warpage;
modulus, strain at break, flexural response and optional differential scanning calorimetry provide supporting
evidence. Block-aware analysis, interaction modelling, confidence intervals, Pareto comparison and Derringer -
Suich desirability are specified to balance mechanical performance against dimensional error. Because student
measurements are not yet  available, the Results and Discussion section is a critical engineering synthesis of
verified literature rather than a report of local test values. The framework provides a practical basis for
determining whether low -cost support can preserve useful annealing effects while limiting geometric loss in
tolerance-sensitive FFF components.
Keywords: additive manufacturing; fused filament fabrication; polylactic acid; annealing; dimensional
stability; mechanical properties; design of experiments; multi-response optimization

1. Introduction
Material extrusion deposits softened thermoplastic roads along programmed toolpaths. Successive roads must
consolidate while cooling, so bead placement is a transient welding problem rather than simple geometric
stacking. ISO/ASTM 52900 defines the terminology and ISO 17295 provides the orientation system [1], [2]. At
interfaces, contact, wetting and molecular interdiffusion compete with cooling; at part scale, raster and build
direction establish preferred load paths [9]-[17].
PLA is accessible and comparatively easy to print, but its thermal response depends on stereochemistry, molar
mass, moisture, additives, pigment and prior cooling [18]-[21]. An FFF coupon is a mesostructured assembly of
roads, interfaces, voids and frozen orientation, not homogeneous bulk PLA. Identical nominal annealing
conditions can therefore produce different outcomes across grades and print strategies.
Annealing is attractive because it can be implemented with relatively simple equipment. Heating above the
glass-transition region increases segmental mobility, enabling stress relaxation, further crystallization and
structural rearrangement. Depending on formulation and thermal history, these mechanisms may increase
useful stiffness, thermal stability or inter -road cohesion [28]-[37]. They also permit the part to move. Length,
width and thickness may change in different directions; out -of-plane curvature may grow; and a mechanically
stronger coupon may no longer meet a drawing tolerance. Mechanical improvement and dimensional fidelity
must therefore be treated as coupled responses rather than independent goals.
Support-assisted annealing modifies the mechanical boundary condition during heating and cooling. Granular
media can provide distributed resistance around complex surfaces, whereas a mechanical fixture applies contact
at designed locations. Published approaches include sand, ceramic powder moulds, salt and gypsum moulds,
liquid baths, elastomeric encapsulation and related methods [38] -[48]. These processes are not thermally
equivalent. Support below melting is a constrained annealing treatment; salt treatment near melting can become
a remoulding or densification process and must not be interpreted as the same mechanism. Support can reduce
gross movement, but it can also change heating lag, friction, surface cleanliness, contact stress and cooling rate.
The central research question is: can support -assisted, sub -melting annealing improve or retain useful
mechanical performance in FFF -printed commercial PLA while reducing the dimensional distortion produced
by otherwise identical unconstrained annealing? Secondary questions concern temperature, duration, support
method, interaction effects, statistical significance, practical effect size and industrial feasibility. The paper
addresses these questions through a literature -supported two-stage design with one controlled print condition,
paired oven-run blocks, three-axis metrology, tensile and flexural testing, and multi -response optimization. Its
value lies in a comparable decision framework: constrained annealing itself has precedent, but a common low -
temperature comparison of unconstrained treatment, multiple granular supports and a quantified fixture remains
limited.
1.1 Research questions
1. How do annealing temperature and hold duration influence signed dimensional change, warpage, tensile
strength, tensile modulus and strain at break in one controlled FFF PLA process?
2. Does physical support reduce dimensional distortion relative to unconstrained annealing at the same measured
thermal exposure?
3. Which of sand, fine sodium chloride and a mechanical fixture offers the most useful combination of restraint,
repeatability, cleanliness and safety at a common screening condition?
4. Do temperature x duration and constraint x thermal -condition interactions change the mechanical -dimensional
trade-off?
5. Can a Pareto -efficient and independently confirmed processing window be identified without accepting a
condition that violates a dimensional or safety limit?
6. How can a low-cost procedure be made reproducible in an undergraduate material-extrusion laboratory?
1.2 Contribution of the study
The contribution is a comparable decision framework, not a universal heat -treatment recipe. It fixes print
architecture, separates a support screen from the factorial, distinguishes specimens from oven -run replicates,
retains signed three -axis responses and requires confirmation. If no method passes the screen, that bounded
negative result still defines the limits of low-cost restraint for the tested PLA.

2. Literature Review
2.1 FDM/FFF additive manufacturing
FFF and FDM commonly denote material extrusion of thermoplastic filament. A drive feeds filament into a hot
end, and the nozzle deposits softened roads as perimeters, infill and successive layers. The part is an
accumulation of roads, so its dimensional and mechanical performance depends on the temperature -time
history of each road and interface [9]-[11].

Figure 1. Original schematic of filament feeding, road deposition and layer-by-layer material extrusion. Not to scale.
2.2 PLA material behaviour
PLA grades vary in stereochemistry and molecular architecture. D -lactide content, chain length, branching,
nucleation, plasticization and degradation history influence transitions, crystallization and mechanics [18]-[20].
Commercial filament can also contain undisclosed pigments or modifiers, while moisture promotes hydrolysis
during heating. A valid study therefore retains one documented lot, controls storage and avoids importing
thermal constants from another formulation.

2.3 FDM process parameters
Layer height, nozzle temperature, orientation, raster, speed, cooling, infill and shell count alter voids and load
transfer. Chacon et al. reported orientation -dependent layer-height and feed -rate effects [22]; Lanzotti et al.
quantified layer, infill and perimeter contributions [23]; and Rajpurohit and Dave examined raster and layer
variables [24]. Build orientation and infill effects can be strong [25], and strength, modulus and toughness need
not share an optimum [26], [27]. Printing parameters must therefore be controlled inputs.

2.4 Interlayer bonding and anisotropy
A deposited road must conform to the previous layer and develop molecular interpenetration. Bellehumeur et
al. modelled neck growth [12], Sun et al. linked processing to bond quality [13], and Coogan and Kazmer
separated wetting, contact and diffusion [14]. Thermal studies show how rapidly weld temperature falls [15],
while PLA work connects cyclic temperature, coalescence and residual alignment to interface strength [16],
[17]. Annealing may relax orientation, but cannot be assumed to remove every void or raster-induced
anisotropy.
2.5 PLA thermal behaviour
Printed PLA contains amorphous material, orientation and some crystalline order. Reheating enables segmental
motion, possible cold crystallization and crystal reorganization [20], [28] -[32]. Crystalline form also matters
[29]; prior processing changes kinetics [30]; and time, temperature, formulation and nucleation interact [31],
[32]. Annealing response is therefore conditional rather than monotonic.

Figure 2. Competing molecular and geometric responses during sub-melting PLA annealing. Original technical schematic.
2.6 Annealing of PLA
FFF studies connect post -print heating with crystallinity, stress, interfaces and dimensions. Wach et al.
combined structural and mechanical evidence [33]; Lee et al. studied stress relaxation and cooling [34]; Butt
and Bhaskar reported material -dependent responses [35]; and von Windheim et al. separated porosity,
crystallinity and adhesion [36]. Grade and orientation alter whether strength, stiffness or heat resistance
improves [32], [37]. The thermal regime and material must therefore be identified explicitly.
2.7 Mechanical effects of annealing
Stress relaxation may reduce frozen stress, crystallization may raise stiffness and interfacial mobility may
improve selected contacts. Conversely, crystallinity can restrict ductility, hydrolysis can lower molecular mass,
and shape change alters the section used for stress [28]-[37]. Post-treatment width and thickness must therefore
be measured, and strength, modulus and strain at break reported separately.
2.8 Annealing-induced dimensional distortion
Dimensional movement combines orientation recovery, residual -stress release, thermal expansion,
crystallization-related volume change, gravity and friction. Raster architecture makes it anisotropic: one axis
can contract while thickness grows, and gradients can bow the part. Stojkovic et al. evaluated tensile strength
and dimensional accuracy together [42], while other PLA studies show condition -, orientation - and grade -
dependence [37], [43], [44]. Length alone can hide width, thickness or flatness failure;  three signed axes and
warpage are required.

2.9 Constrained and support-assisted annealing
Support-assisted evidence includes oven, sand, water and glycerol treatments for tough PLA [38], ceramic -
powder moulds for PLA and ABS [39], and salt or gypsum moulds in regimes that can approach melting [40],
[41]. Further work compares oven and sand for filled systems [45], performs multi -parameter optimization
[46], uses salt or elastomeric containment [47], and studies sand-embedded high-heat PLA [48].
The support physics differ. Sand is conformable, but packing and moisture affect restraint and heating lag. Fine
salt packs closely, yet is hygroscopic, corrosive when damp and distinct from near -melt remoulding. A fixture
is reusable and measurable, but excessive load or mismatched expansion can imprint the surface or create
stress. Screening is more defensible than assuming one method is best.

2.10 Research gap
Supported annealing is not unstudied. The gap is comparability: published work varies PLA formulation,
geometry, orientation, thermal regime, support physics and response definition, and some salt processes
approach melting. The targeted literature did not identify one peer -reviewed study that screens sand, salt and a
quantified fixture against unconstrained sub -melting treatment in one commercial PLA, then uses a replicated
temperature x duration factorial with signed three -axis metrology, warpage, tensile  response, block -aware
statistics, multi-response optimization and confirmation.
3. Research Objectives
3.1 Primary objective
To evaluate whether physical support during sub -melting annealing can improve or retain useful mechanical
performance of FFF -printed commercial PLA while reducing three -axis dimensional distortion and warpage
relative to unconstrained annealing.
3.2 Secondary objectives
7. Explain the thermal, crystallization, stress-relaxation and interfacial mechanisms relevant to annealed FFF PLA.
8. Compare as-printed, unconstrained, sand-supported, salt-supported and gap-controlled fixture conditions at 95 C
for 60 min.
9. Quantify temperature, hold-time and constraint effects at 80, 95 and 110 C for 30, 60 and 90 min.
10. Measure tensile strength, tensile modulus, strain at break, signed length/width/thickness change and out -of-
plane warpage.
11. Use a limited three -group flexural comparison to check whether the tensile -based decision transfers to
bending.
12. Separate treatment effects from print -plate, oven -run and measurement variation through randomization,
blocking and repeatability checks.
13. Develop a transparent multi -response decision that maximizes useful strength and stiffness while
minimizing dimensional error and warpage.
14. Assess low-cost implementation, contamination, cleaning, thermal lag, fixture reuse and laboratory safety
for FFF applications.

5. Materials and Methods
5.1 Material
The material is commercial neat PLA filament, nominally 1.75 mm, from one colour and production lot. The
actual trade name, lot, source, storage history and supplier thermal limits are recorded at procurement rather
than guessed. Diameter is checked at distributed positions with a calibrated micrometer. The lot is stored sealed
and dry; any approved drying procedure is applied uniformly. A new lot is not pooled unless it is deliberately
modelled as a block.
5.2 FDM process
One calibrated material -extrusion system is used, with its model and firmware recorded from the laboratory
machine. Controlled settings are a 0.40 mm brass nozzle, 210 C nozzle, 60 C plate, 0.20 mm layer, 100%
rectilinear/lines infill, alternating +45/-45 degree raster, 45 mm s-1 nominal speed, flat XY orientation, constant
validated cooling, three perimeters and five solid top/bottom layers. The slicer release, profile, CAD, mesh and
G-code are archived.

5.3 Specimen geometry
Tensile coupons follow ASTM D638 -22 Type I where machine and build capacity permit: 165 mm minimum
overall length, 13 mm narrow width, 57 mm narrow length, 50 mm gauge length, 115 mm nominal grip
separation and 3.2 mm nominal thickness [4]. If Type IV is necessary, it is adopted for every tensile group
before printing. ASTM D790 -25 flexural bars use a nominal 127 x 12.7 x 3.2 mm planning geometry [5].
Actual measured sections govern stress calculations. All coupons are flat XY and reported using ISO
17295:2023 [2].
5.4 Printing and specimen allocation
Identifiers are assigned before printing, and conditions are distributed across plates and randomized positions
so treatment is not identical to print batch. Records retain plate ID, position, time, operator, ambient conditions
and defects. Removal and stabilization are consistent. A failed print is documented and replaced with a new
identifier, never silently excluded.

5.5 Specimen conditioning
Specimens are conditioned under ASTM D618 -21 Procedure A for at least 40 h at 23 +/ - 2 C and 50 +/ - 10%
relative humidity where applicable [6]. The same atmosphere is used after treatment and during testing. Time,
atmosphere and deviations are recorded because PLA dimensions and properties are moisture- and temperature-
sensitive.
5.6 Baseline measurements
Before heating, overall length, gauge width/thickness, mass and maximum out -of-plane warpage are measured
at fixed locations. ASTM D5947 -24 guides solid -plastic dimensions [7]. Instrument resolution must be small
relative to the expected change. Three repeated placements of representative coupons establish short -term
metrology repeatability and standardize measurement force and orientation.

5.7 Annealing setup
A dedicated ventilated oven receives a spatial-uniformity check. A calibrated probe/logger is placed beside the
coupon or in a sacrificial support assembly. After the empty oven stabilizes, the tray is loaded and the hold
starts only when the specimen environment returns within +/ - 2 C of target. The log retains heat -up delay,
temperature range, excursions and door openings. Treatments are 80, 95 or 110 C for 30, 60 or 90 min [37],
[42], [43], [46]. The 110 C level proceeds only after a sacrificial coupon s hows no sticking, collapse, smoke or
abnormal odour.
After the hold, the hot tray is moved to a heat -resistant surface. The specimen remains constrained until the
adjacent probe is below 50 C, then is released and cleaned. Cooling location, release time and movement are
recorded. Early release can permit distortion, while slow cooling can alter crystallization; measured specimen
temperature, not display setpoint alone, defines exposure.

5.8 Sand support
Low-dust graded sand is dried to constant mass at 105 +/ - 5 C and cooled sealed. A lidded heat -stable tray
receives a 20 mm base; the oriented specimen is covered by at least 20 mm and given one fixed gentle vibration
cycle without tamping the gauge. Grade, mass, fill height, vibration and clearance are recorded because packing
and thermal lag are part of the process.
5.9 Salt support
Fine NaCl is screened only as dry support below melting. It is dried to constant mass at 105 +/ - 5 C, cooled
sealed, and packed in a dedicated corrosion-resistant tray with the same 20 mm base/cover depths. Grade, mass,
fill and moisture history are recorded. After cooling, salt is removed without prolonged water exposure and
cleaning is documented. This differs explicitly from near -melting remoulding [40], [41]; damp salt and
vulnerable metals are avoided.
5.10 Mechanical fixture
Two flat, thermally stable plates use PTFE release films and four symmetric fasteners. Precision stops set the
hot gap to measured specimen thickness plus 0.05 mm, limiting bow without intentional gauge compression.
Fasteners seat against the stops diagonally; plate material, flatness, stop thickness and seating -force calibration
are recorded. Each run is inspected for expansion, bow, imprinting and edge contact. Gap control is more
reproducible than arbitrary torque.

5.11 Experimental matrix
Stage 1 screens AP, U, sand, salt and fixture conditions at n=5; annealed groups use 95 C/60 min. Selection
applies safety and feasibility gates before dimensional error, warpage, tensile retention, variability and
cleaning. Stage 2 is a 3 x 3 x 2 factorial of temperature, duration and U versus selected constraint, n=5 per cell.
AP and the applicable 95 C/60 min screen groups are reused with full traceability.

5.12 Tensile testing
ASTM D638-22 tensile tests use a calibrated UTM, suitable grips, load cell and extensometer [4]. Type I speed
is 5 mm min-1 unless the complete method requires otherwise. Post-treatment gauge width/thickness define the
initial area. Report ultimate strength, extensometer modulus, strain at break, break location and failure mode.
Crosshead travel does not replace extensometer strain for modulus; invalid failures follow a written rule and
remain traceable.
5.13 Flexural testing
ASTM D790-25 flexure uses three -point loading [5]. Actual mean depth determines the nominal 16:1 support
span and test rate. Report the deflection method because crosshead and deflectometer values differ. AP,
thermally matched U and the selected condition are compared at n=5; this confirms transfer to bending rather
than repeating the full optimization.

5.14 Dimensional measurement
After cooling and reconditioning, all dimensions, mass and warpage are remeasured at identical locations.
Calibration and resolution are recorded. Duplicate readings are taken independently and a third resolves
disagreement beyond repeatability. Report signed axis changes with E_RMS or E_tol, and report warpage with
its reference plane and placement repeatability.
5.15 Optional thermal and microscopic characterization
Optional DSC of representative AP, U and selected-support samples follows ASTM D3418-21 with mass, pan,
atmosphere, rate and cycle reported [8]. First heating captures the print/anneal history; a later heating is not the
original state. Report transitions and integration limits. For neat PLA, crystallinity may use the commonly
adopted 93.0 J g-1 fully crystalline reference [21], with baseline and crystal-form uncertainty stated.
X_c (%) = [(Delta H_m - Delta H_cc) / (w_PLA x Delta H_m^0)] x 100 (4)
Here Delta H_m is melting enthalpy, Delta H_cc is cold -crystallization enthalpy expressed as a positive
magnitude, w_PLA is PLA mass fraction and Delta H_m^0 is the declared fully crystalline reference. No
crystallinity value is calculated without genuine DSC data. Optical microscopy or SEM may compare voids,
fracture surfaces and inter -road interfaces only when preparation, magnification, scale bars and sampling
locations are documented.

5.18 Statistical analysis
Report individual data, mean, standard deviation, coefficient of variation and two -sided 95% confidence
intervals. Stage 1 compares each support with U using multiplicity control and effect sizes. The definitive
--- PAGE 16 ---

16
model includes temperature, duration, constraint, required interactions, curvature and oven run as a
block/random effect. Paired U -supported coupons improve the constraint contrast; print plate and position
remain nuisance terms. Randomization, replication and blocking follow DOE principles [49].
Predeclared tests use alpha = 0.05 with residual, normal -quantile and influence diagnostics. Transformation,
heteroscedastic models or robust sensitivity checks require justification. Report exact p -values with effects and
intervals. Statistical significance is not manufacturing importance: a dimension outside tolerance or
unacceptable warpage fails regardless of strength. Five independent specimens per cell mainly resolve large
effects; add blocks if key contrasts remain imprecise.
5.19 Safety and feasibility
Essential equipment is a calibrated FFF printer, PLA lot, ventilated oven, independent logger, calibrated
caliper/micrometer, surface plate and warpage indicator, balance, UTM with extensometer, heat -stable trays
and support/fixture hardware. A flexural fixture is strongly recommended. DSC, TGA, microscopy, SEM and
profilometry are optional.
Use thermal gloves, tongs, eye protection and heat -resistant surfaces. Smoke, sticking, strong odour or
abnormal colour stops oven work. Control sand dust; keep salt dry and away from vulnerable metals; guard
fixture pinch points. Mechanical testing requires correct grips/load cell, eye protection and a fragment
exclusion zone. Local DTU risk assessment and supervisor instructions take precedence.
6. Results and Discussion - Literature-Based Engineering Analysis
This section analyses published experiments to establish mechanisms, feasible conditions and competing
trends. Cross -study numerical comparison is limited by different PLA grades, geometries, orientations and
response definitions. Any numerical value is explicitly source -attributed and is not a measurement of the
planned DTU coupons.
6.1 Effect of annealing temperature
Temperature controls the rate of relaxation and crystallization. At 80 C, many commercial grades are mobile
without approaching melting; gross distortion may be lower, but short holds may not strongly crystallize a slow
grade. Even modest treatment can alter dimensions while overall form is retained [34], [37], [42], [43].
At 95 C, mobility is greater and the level is represented in extended-time and optimization work [43], [46]; it is
a discriminating screen condition, not a declared optimum. At 110 C, crystallization may accelerate, but so can
stress release, contact marking and gradients. Kahya et al. tested 70-110 C and 40-200 min, reporting 90 C/120
min as favourable for their material [46]. This supports a central region while showing why another grade
cannot inherit that optimum.
A non -linear response is expected: selected properties may improve initially, while dimensional error
accelerates with mobility. Three levels can reveal a middle compromise that two levels miss. An unsafe 110 C
pilot requires a revised safe window, not continued testing for statistical completeness.
6.2 Effect of annealing duration
Duration is measured after the specimen environment reaches setpoint, because supports create thermal lag.
Thirty minutes may allow early relaxation with limited movement; 60 min is a practical screen centre; and 90
min tests continued change or saturation.
Longer is not always better: crystallization can plateau while distortion or degradation risk accumulates.
Extended 55-95 C treatments show orientation and grade dependence [43], and nucleation alters response [32].
Duration must therefore interact with temperature and support-dependent heating lag.
6.3 Effect on tensile strength
Tensile findings are mixed because interface rearrangement and stress relief can improve load transfer, while
voids, degradation or restricted ductility can reduce performance [33] -[37], [44]. Annealing also changes the
stress denominator; using nominal rather than measured post -treatment dimensions can create a false strength
increase.
--- PAGE 17 ---

17
Wijnbergen et al. compared four media and found method- and orientation-dependent deformation and strength
[38]. Sand produced the least ring deformation: 2.04% inner -diameter shrinkage and 1.99% height increase for
horizontal rings. These source -specific, opposing signs show why one -dimensional shrinkage is inadequate.
Other mould and sand studies also find containment -dependent mechanics, with limited transfer across grades
and regimes [39], [41], [45].
6.4 Effect on tensile modulus and ductility
Modulus can rise with crystalline order while strength remains controlled by voids and fracture paths; strain at
break may fall as local strain redistribution becomes harder [28], [29], [32]. This matters for jigs: stiffness can
improve positioning while brittleness worsens clamping or impact behaviour.
Extensometer strain is required for modulus, and ductility needs a common validity rule. A specimen that
breaks outside the gauge after fixture imprinting is not evidence of improved bulk PLA. Failure locations must
accompany summary statistics.
6.5 Dimensional shrinkage and warpage
Dimensional change is structural recovery. Nozzle flow, toolpath turning, flattening and non -uniform cooling
leave direction-dependent orientation and stress. Reheating can contract a preferred road direction while width
or thickness grows; crystallization adds volume change, and gradients, gravity and friction convert recovery
into curvature.
Printing and annealing variables jointly affect tensile and dimensional response [42], while orientation,
architecture and grade can reverse trends [37], [43], [44]. A specimen may retain length while bowing or
changing section. Delta L, Delta W, Delta t, combined error and direct warpage are therefore all retained.
6.6 Effect of sand support
Sand provides distributed, conformal restraint without a custom mould. It minimized deformation for the
tough-PLA ring study [38]; powder restraint is also plausible [39]; and oven -versus-sand response depends on
composite formulation [45]. These findings justify screening sand but do not prove superiority for neat PLA
coupons.
Granular support also varies with particle size, moisture, mass, cover depth and vibration. Denser packing may
restrain more but delay heating or create pressure gradients; dust and cleaning add burden. The medium,
packing and measured specimen temperature must therefore be recorded.
6.7 Effect of salt support
Dry fine salt fills small gaps and is inexpensive. Published methods show that salt containment can preserve
shape or support more severe treatment [40], [41], [47], but some approach melting or autoclave conditions.
Their mechanics do not transfer directly; this screen asks only whether dry salt restrains sub -melting
movement.
Salt absorbs moisture, contaminates surfaces, corrodes when damp and may require washing that changes
conditioning. It should advance only if repeatable benefit justifies dedicated drying, trays and cleaning; a small
geometric gain with unreliable moisture history is not industrially attractive.
6.8 Effect of mechanical constraint
A fixture provides a defined reusable boundary: plates suppress bow, stops define the gap, and release films
permit in-plane slip. It can be calibrated and rapidly reused. Mould and encapsulation studies exist [39], [41],
[47], but a low-temperature comparison adding a quantified fixture to sand, salt and U remains limited.
Over-restraint can store stress, buckle edges, emboss surfaces or transfer fixture expansion into PLA. Poor plate
flatness can exchange global warp for local thickness error. Gap control avoids intentional compression, and
imprint/contact damage fails the feasibility gate. Flatness alone cannot select the fixture if tensile scatter or
surface integrity worsens.

7. Multi-Response Optimization Framework
Tensile strength alone is an incomplete decision: a stronger part may exceed tolerance, warp or become brittle,
while minimum distortion may come from an ineffective thermal cycle. The objectives therefore maximize
useful strength/stiffness and minimize E_RMS or E_tol, warpage and shrinkage. Strain at break has a lower -
bound desirability; safety, sticking, collapse and surface damage are feasibility gates.
Each response y_i is converted to desirability d_i from 0 (unacceptable) to 1 (target). Maximize functions rise
from a minimum acceptable value; minimize functions fall toward a maximum tolerable value. Limits, weights
and curve shapes are set before outcome review under the Derringer-Suich formulation [50].
D = [product(d_i ^ w_i)] ^ [1 / sum(w_i)] (5)
Here d_i is an individual desirability and w_i its predeclared weight. The geometric mean makes D zero when
any response is unacceptable, so strength cannot hide dimensional failure. Report D with each d_i and raw
units; vary reasonable weights and limits to test selection stability.

Pareto analysis complements D. A condition is dominated when another is no worse on every response and
better on one. The efficient set exposes the strength -dimensional trade-off; selection occurs only after safety
and tolerance gates. Literature suggests a central thermal region and controlled fixture or sand restraint may be
favourable, but this is a testable expectation, not an optimum claim.

8. Industrial Significance
A reliable support -assisted cycle could extend the usefulness of PLA in functional prototypes, assembly aids,
jigs, fixtures, drill or inspection guides, low -volume tooling, replacement parts and components exposed to
moderate heat. These applications value final geometry and repeatability as strongly as coupon strength. A jig
that becomes stiffer but moves a locating surface outside tolerance has failed its purpose. The proposed
response hierarchy reflects this reality by treating mechanical performance an d dimensional accuracy
simultaneously.
8.1 Advantages of constrained annealing
The principal attraction is a potentially low-cost post-process that uses existing FFF equipment and a controlled
oven. Distributed or fixture restraint may reduce gross movement, improve part -to-part consistency, limit
redesign iterations and make thermal conditioning more practical for tolerance -sensitive parts. Sand and salt
can conform to irregular forms without machining a mould. A fixture can be reused and integrated with
inspection for repeated geometries. The method also creates a disciplined qualif ication route: define the
drawing need, screen boundary conditions, measure the thermal cycle, model a safe window and independently
confirm the selected condition.

Transfer beyond coupons requires restraint geometry and thermal lag to be reconsidered. Thick ribs, cavities

9. Limitations
The framework deliberately focuses on one commercial neat PLA lot, one printer, one slicer profile, flat
coupon geometries and one raster architecture. PLA grades differ widely, so conclusions cannot automatically
extend to tough, nucleated, high -heat, recycled, pigmented or filled products. Coupon response does not fully
represent thick walls, sharp thickness transitions, enclosed cavities, assemblies or topology -optimized forms.
The 80-110 C range is scientifically plausible but still requires a sacrifici al safety pilot for the actual filament
and oven.
Five independent specimens per cell are feasible for an undergraduate project but provide limited power for
small interactions. Additional oven-run blocks may be necessary if residual variation is high. Oven uniformity,
time to specimen setpoint, granular packing and fixture flatness can introduce variation even with a written
method. The support screen selects one method for the factorial, so the definitive design does not estimate the
complete temperature dependence of every support. Flexural testing is c onfirmatory and does not replace
fatigue, creep, impact or heat-deflection testing.
Without DSC or microscopy, crystallization and interface explanations remain literature-supported mechanisms
rather than direct measurements in the tested coupons. Even with DSC, a small cut sample may not represent
through-thickness or between-road variation. Short-term post-treatment dimensions do not establish long -term
aging, moisture response or creep under service load. Finally, no local effect estimate, p -value or optimum can
be stated until specimens have been produced and tested under the documented procedure.
10. Future Scope
The most useful next step after the coupon study is geometry -dependent validation. Plates, rings, ribs, holes,
bosses and mixed -thickness components should be annealed under the selected method and inspected with
coordinate metrology or structured-light scanning. A dimensional compensation model could then relate stable
signed shrinkage to CAD oversizing: print the predicted pre -compensated geometry, apply the controlled
annealing cycle and confirm the final drawing dimensions. This extension should be eval uated with prediction
intervals and independent parts before it is used as a manufacturing rule.
Material extensions include PETG, ABS, ASA, nylon, fibre -filled PLA, high -heat PLA and recycled
formulations. Process extensions include constraint pressure or gap as a designed factor, heat -transfer
modelling inside granular media, topology -optimized fixtures, controlled heating/cooling ramps, digital image
correlation, computed tomography, heat-deflection testing, fatigue, creep and environmental aging. DSC, TGA,
SEM and fracture microscopy could connect macroscopic response with crystallinity, degradatio n, voids and
road interfaces. Response -surface or Bayesian sequential design may refine a safe window after the present
factorial establishes curvature and interaction structure; machine-learning prediction is appropriate only when a
sufficiently large, traceable, geometry-diverse dataset exists.
11. Conclusion
Annealing of FFF-printed PLA is a coupled thermal, structural and geometric process. Heating above the glass-
transition region increases chain mobility and can promote stress relaxation, crystallization and changes at
inter-road interfaces. Those mechanisms may improve heat resistance, stiffness or selected mechanical
properties, but they also release the frozen orientation and residual stresses created during deposition. The
--- PAGE 21 ---

21
resulting contraction, transverse growth and warpage can make a nominally stronger component unsuitable for
a tolerance -sensitive application. The literature therefore supports evaluating mechanical performance and
dimensional stability together rather than ranking conditions by tensile strength alone.
Physical support offers a plausible means of controlling that trade -off, but sand, salt and mechanical fixtures
impose different boundary conditions. Sand is conformable and inexpensive, yet its packing and thermal lag
must be controlled. Salt can provide fine granular restraint but introduces moisture, corrosion and cleaning
concerns and must be distinguished from near -melting salt remoulding. A gap -controlled fixture provides the
most explicit and reusable geometric restraint for planar specimens, while r isking contact imprint, stored stress
and thermal-expansion mismatch. Existing studies demonstrate that supported treatments are feasible; they do
not provide one universal best method because formulation, geometry, orientation and thermal cycle differ.
The research design resolves this uncertainty in two stages. A 25 -specimen centre-condition screen compares
AP, U, sand, salt and fixture boundary conditions at 95 C for 60 min. The selected feasible support then enters
a replicated 80/95/110 C x 30/60/90 min factorial against U. Reuse of the appropriate screen coupons, followed
by new tensile and flexural confirmation groups, gives 130 physical specimens. Standards -based specimen
conditioning, measured three -axis dimensions, direct warpage, extensometer te nsile testing, block -aware
models, confidence intervals and confirmation protect the comparison from nominal -dimension errors and
oven-cycle pseudoreplication.
The multi-response framework is central to the engineering decision. Pareto analysis retains the visible trade -
off, while predeclared Derringer-Suich desirability combines acceptable mechanical and dimensional responses
without allowing a severe failure to be hidden by an average score. No local optimum or property improvement
is asserted before testing. Instead, the completed paper provides a technically defensible method and a
literature-grounded expectation that an intermediate thermal exposure, combined  with controlled physical
restraint, may offer a more useful balance than either mild untreated behaviour or severe unconstrained heating.
Execution of the documented experiments is required before the condition can be recommended for DTU
laboratory or industrial use.

Appendix B. Specimen Identification Scheme
Identifiers use STAGE -CONDITION-TEMPERATURE-DURATION-REPLICATE. Stage codes are S
(screen), D (definitive), C (tensile confirmation) and F (flexural confirmation). Condition codes are AP, U,
SAND, SALT, FIX and SC. Temperatures use T080, T095 or T110; durations use M030, M060 or M090; and
replicates use R01-R05. AP identifiers omit thermal fields. Examples are S -SAND-T095-M060-R02 and D-U-
T080-M030-R04. The final support name remains in the original screen ID, while SC is the analysis alias used
for the selected method in the paired factorial.
Specimen ID alone does not establish independence. Separate fields retain print plate, oven run, positions,
operator and machine-data filename. Labels stay outside the gauge section without notching. Replaced IDs are
retired with the failure reason and are never recycled.
